Finding the Right Time to Call Someone in Another Country

📅 August 27, 2026  ·  ⏱ 5 min read  ·  🏷 Time Zones, Guides

There's a particular kind of awkwardness that comes with scheduling a call across countries. You propose a time, the other person agrees, and then one of you realizes it's 7 a.m. their time. Or worse, you both show up an hour apart because one country moved to summer time and the other didn't.

The good news is that most of this pain comes from a few predictable habits, and they're easy to fix.

Start with the actual gap

Before you suggest anything, find out how many hours separate you. A quick search for "time in [city]" tells you the current local time there. Once you know the gap — say, your counterpart is six hours ahead — you can stop guessing and start counting.

Look for the overlap, not the perfect hour

You don't need a time that's convenient for both of you. You need a window where you're both reasonably awake. If you're in New York and they're in London, your 9 a.m. is their 2 p.m. — fine for a working call. If they're in Tokyo, your morning is their late evening, so your evening is their morning. Write down the two or three hours that work on both sides and pick from those.

Watch the season

Summer time (daylight saving) starts and ends on different dates in different places, and some countries don't use it at all. The gap that worked in January can be off by an hour in July. If a standing meeting keeps feeling "off" by sixty minutes, this is usually why.

A few things that make life easier

  • Send the invite in the other person's time zone, or include both. "Tuesday 3 p.m. your time / 10 a.m. mine" removes all doubt.
  • Keep a small note of the time difference for the people you talk to often. It sounds trivial and saves you every single week.
  • For regular catch-ups across a big gap, accept that one of you will sometimes take the early or late slot. Trade it week to week so it's fair.
  • Record the call when you can't find a live slot that suits everyone. A five-minute voice note beats a missed conversation.

Tools help, but they don't decide

There are plenty of meeting planners that draw the overlap for you. They're handy for the visual, but the real decision is human: who's willing to shift, and by how much. A short message asking "would mornings or evenings suit you better?" gets you further than any calculator.

The goal isn't to find a perfect time. It's to find a good enough one, confirm it clearly, and move on.
