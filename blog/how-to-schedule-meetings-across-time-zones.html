Step 1: Know Everyone's Time Zone

Before you can schedule anything, you need to know where everyone is — not just their city, but their current UTC offset. This matters because the same city can have different offsets during DST vs. standard time, and some regions don't observe DST at all.

Step 2: Find the Overlap

Plot everyone's working hours on a 24-hour UTC timeline. The overlap is your scheduling window:

  • New York (9-5 EST) = 14:00-22:00 UTC
  • London (9-5 GMT) = 09:00-17:00 UTC
  • Overlap: 14:00-17:00 UTC (3 hours)

For teams spanning US + Asia, the overlap may be as small as 1-2 hours — or nonexistent.

Step 3: Choose the Right Tool

Don't do time zone math manually. Use tools like our meeting planner to visualize overlaps, or World Time Buddy for side-by-side comparisons.

Step 4: Write a Clear Invitation

A good cross-timezone meeting invitation includes the time in UTC, the time in each attendee's local time (with city names), and a link to a time zone converter.

Step 5: Handle DST Transitions

Daylight saving time changes can shift meeting times by an hour. After a DST change, send a confirmation: "Our 10 AM EST meeting is now 11 AM EDT." Use calendar tools that auto-adjust for DST.

Meeting Etiquette for Global Teams

  1. Rotate the pain: Don't always ask the same person to take the early/late call
  2. Record meetings: So those who can't attend live can catch up
  3. Be punctual: If the meeting is at 6 AM for someone, start on time
  4. Keep it short: Inconvenient-time meetings should be focused and efficient
  5. Respect local holidays: Don't schedule meetings on someone's national holiday

When There's No Good Overlap

For teams with no overlapping business hours (e.g., US West Coast + East Asia), consider async standups (recorded video updates), split meetings (two shorter sessions), or written updates via Slack/email.
