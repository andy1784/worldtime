How Daylight Saving Time Affects Your Health

📅 2026-06-25  ·  ⏱ 5 min read  ·  🏷 Health & Science

Twice a year, about 1.5 billion people adjust their clocks by one hour. Most of us shrug it off as a minor inconvenience. But research shows the health effects of these transitions are real — and more significant than you might think.

The Spring Forward: Losing an Hour

When clocks spring forward in March (in the Northern Hemisphere), most people lose about 40-60 minutes of sleep for one or two nights. That doesn't sound catastrophic, but the body notices.

Studies have found:

  • Heart attacks increase by about 24% on the Monday after spring forward
  • Stroke rates rise by about 8% in the two days after the transition
  • Workplace injuries increase, likely due to fatigue and reduced alertness
  • Car accidents spike by about 6% on the Monday after the time change

The effect is strongest in the first few days and generally fades within a week as the body adjusts.

The Fall Back: Gaining an Hour

The autumn transition is gentler. Gaining an hour of sleep sounds like a gift, but it still disrupts your circadian rhythm. People often wake up earlier than intended and have trouble falling asleep at their usual time.

Interestingly, studies show a small decrease in heart attacks after the fall transition — the opposite of spring. But the disruption to sleep patterns still affects mood, concentration, and productivity for several days.

Why Does a One-Hour Shift Matter?

Your body runs on a circadian rhythm — an internal clock that regulates sleep, hormone release, body temperature, and metabolism. This rhythm is primarily set by light exposure.

When you shift your clock, you don't shift the sun. If you used to wake up at 7 AM in daylight, after spring forward you're waking up at 7 AM when it's still dark. Your body thinks it's an hour earlier than the clock says.

This "social jet lag" — the mismatch between your biological clock and your social schedule — is what causes most of the health effects.

Who's Most Affected?

  • Night owls struggle more with spring forward because their natural sleep schedule is already later
  • Children and teenagers need more sleep and are more sensitive to disruption
  • People with existing sleep disorders may take longer to adjust
  • Shift workers face compounded disruption on top of already irregular schedules

How to Minimize the Impact

You can't avoid the time change, but you can prepare:

  1. Shift gradually. Starting 3-4 days before the change, go to bed and wake up 15 minutes earlier (for spring) or later (for fall)
  2. Get morning sunlight. Exposure to natural light in the morning helps reset your circadian rhythm faster
  3. Avoid caffeine after 2 PM in the days around the transition
  4. Don't nap excessively. A short nap is fine, but long naps can make it harder to sleep at night

The Bigger Question

More countries are questioning whether DST is worth the health cost. The European Union voted to abolish seasonal time changes in 2019 (though implementation has been delayed). Several US states have passed legislation to adopt permanent DST, pending federal approval.

The debate comes down to this: is the extra evening daylight worth the twice-yearly disruption to millions of people's sleep and health? The science increasingly suggests the costs outweigh the benefits.

If you're traveling during a DST transition, check the current time in your destination — not all countries switch on the same dates.
