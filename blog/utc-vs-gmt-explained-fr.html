UTC vs GMT : Quelle est la différence (et est-ce important) ?

You've seen both terms. UTC time. GMT time. They seem interchangeable — and for most everyday purposes, they are. But there's a real difference, and it matters in some contexts.

What Is GMT?

GMT stands for Greenwich Mean Time. It's based on the mean solar time at the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, London — the location of the prime meridian (0° longitude).

GMT has been used since 1847, when British railways adopted it as a standard. In 1884, the International Meridian Conference established GMT as the world's reference time.

GMT is a time zone. The UK uses GMT in winter and BST (GMT+1) in summer.

What Is UTC?

UTC stands for Coordinated Universal Time. It's a time standard (not a time zone) maintained by a network of over 400 atomic clocks worldwide.

UTC was established in 1960 to provide a more precise and stable reference than GMT, which is based on Earth's rotation (which is gradually slowing down).

UTC never changes. No DST, no adjustments (except occasional leap seconds to account for Earth's slowing rotation).

Does the Difference Matter?

For everyday use — scheduling meetings, checking the time, setting your watch — no. GMT and UTC are the same to within 1 second.

For technical applications — aviation, computing, scientific research — yes. UTC is the standard because it's more precise and stable. Air traffic control, GPS satellites, and computer systems all use UTC.

The term "UTC" was a compromise. English speakers wanted "CUT" (Coordinated Universal Time) and French speakers wanted "TUC" (Temps Universel Coordonné). UTC was the middle ground that satisfied no one linguistically but worked diplomatically.

Leap Seconds

Because Earth's rotation is gradually slowing, UTC occasionally needs a "leap second" to stay in sync with solar time. Since 1972, 27 leap seconds have been added. There's ongoing debate about abolishing leap seconds, which cause headaches for computer systems.

GMT doesn't have leap seconds — it's based on Earth's actual rotation, so it naturally drifts.

Which Should You Use?

For general purposes, either is fine. If you're in the UK, "GMT" is more familiar. If you're in aviation, computing, or science, "UTC" is the standard.

The important thing: when scheduling international meetings, always specify the time zone clearly. "10 AM UTC" is unambiguous. "10 AM GMT" could be misinterpreted as "10 AM UK time" (which might be BST, not GMT, depending on the season).
