The Problem: Railway Time

Before the 19th century, every city kept its own local time based on the sun. Noon was when the sun was highest in the sky — which meant noon in London was 10:05 AM in Bristol. The invention of the railway changed everything. Trains needed schedules, and with every town running on its own solar time, chaos ensued.

British railways solved this by creating "Railway Time" — a single standard time (GMT) used across all stations. By 1847, most British railways had adopted GMT. In 1880, GMT became the legal standard for all of Great Britain.

The Solution: Standard Time Zones

The concept of global time zones is credited to Sir Sandford Fleming, a Canadian railway engineer. In 1879, he proposed dividing the world into 24 time zones, each spanning 15 degrees of longitude (360° ÷ 24 = 15° per zone).

The International Meridian Conference in 1884 in Washington, D.C. formalized this system. Representatives from 25 countries agreed to adopt Greenwich as the prime meridian and divide the world into 24 standard time zones.

The Quirky Cases

Spain's Wrong Time Zone

Spain should geographically be in GMT (like Portugal), but in 1940, Franco changed the clocks to match Nazi Germany's CET (UTC+1). The change was never reversed. Today, Spaniards eat dinner at 10 PM partly because their clocks are an hour ahead of their solar time.

Nepal's 45-Minute Offset

Nepal uses UTC+5:45 — the only country with a 45-minute offset. This was chosen in 1956 to align with Kathmandu's solar time. It's a statement of independence from India's UTC+5:30.

Samoa's Day Skip

In 2011, Samoa moved from the east side of the International Date Line to the west side. They skipped December 30 entirely — going from December 29 to December 31 — to align with trading partners Australia and New Zealand.

UTC: The Modern Standard

Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), established in 1960, is the world's time standard. It's maintained by a network of over 400 atomic clocks worldwide. The term "UTC" is a compromise between English speakers who wanted "CUT" and French speakers who wanted "TUC".
