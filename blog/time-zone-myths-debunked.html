Myth #1: Every Time Zone Is Exactly 1 Hour Apart

Reality: Time zones can differ by 30 or even 45 minutes. India uses UTC+5:30, Nepal uses UTC+5:45, and Australia's Central Time is UTC+9:30. These unusual offsets exist for political and geographical reasons — countries want their standard time to roughly match solar noon.

Myth #2: The International Date Line Is a Straight Line

Reality: The International Date Line zigzags significantly. It bends around Kiribati, which spans both sides of the equator. In 1995, Kiribati moved the date line eastward so the entire country would be on the same day. Samoa and Tokelau skipped an entire day — December 30, 2011 — to move from the east side to the west side of the date line for better trade with Australia and New Zealand.

Myth #3: GMT and UTC Are the Same Thing

Reality: GMT (Greenwich Mean Time) is a time zone, while UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) is a time standard. UTC is maintained by atomic clocks and is accurate to within a nanosecond. GMT is based on Earth's rotation, which is slightly irregular. For most practical purposes they're equivalent, but scientifically they're different. Read our full UTC vs GMT guide for more details.

Myth #4: All of China Uses One Time Zone

Reality: Geographically, China spans five time zones — but politically, it uses only one: China Standard Time (UTC+8). This means in western Xinjiang, the sun may not rise until 10 AM in winter. Some locals unofficially use 'Xinjiang Time' (UTC+6) for daily life, even though official schedules follow Beijing time.

Myth #5: Daylight Saving Time Saves Energy

Reality: The original rationale for DST was energy conservation, but modern studies show minimal savings — and some suggest it actually increases energy use due to higher air conditioning demand. The EU voted to abolish DST in 2021 but implementation has been delayed. The US extended DST by 4 weeks in 2007 with the Energy Policy Act.

Myth #6: Russia Has the Most Time Zones

Reality: Russia has 11 time zones, which is the most of any single country. But France has 12 time zones if you count its overseas territories (French Polynesia, New Caledonia, Réunion, etc.). The UK also has 8 time zones including overseas territories.

Myth #7: Time Zones Follow Straight Lines of Longitude

Reality: Time zone boundaries are political, not geographical. They follow country borders, state lines, and sometimes even county lines. Spain should geographically be in GMT (like Portugal) but uses CET (UTC+1) because Franco aligned it with Germany in 1940.

Myth #8: There Are Exactly 24 Time Zones

Reality: There are actually 38 time zones when you count all the 30-minute and 45-minute offsets. The total offset range is from UTC-12 (Baker Island) to UTC+14 (Line Islands, Kiribati) — a 26-hour span.

Myth #9: Your Phone Handles All Time Zone Issues

Reality: While phones handle basic time zone conversion, they can struggle with edge cases: meetings scheduled across DST transitions, locations that don't observe DST (Arizona, Hawaii), and historical dates. Always double-check critical meetings with a dedicated tool like our meeting planner.

Myth #10: DST Is Observed Worldwide

Reality: Most of Asia and Africa don't observe DST. Japan, China, India, and most of Southeast Asia stay on standard time year-round. In the US, Arizona (except Navajo Nation) and Hawaii don't observe DST. The map of DST observance is surprisingly patchy.

Key Takeaways

  • Time zones are political, not purely geographical
  • Half-hour and 45-minute offsets are more common than you think
  • DST is increasingly controversial and may be phased out
  • Always verify time differences for important calls and meetings
  • The world has 38 time zones, not 24
