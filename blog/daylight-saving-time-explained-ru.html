Переход на летнее время: почему 70 стран возится со своими часами два раза в год

Twice a year, billions of people change their clocks by one hour. In spring, they lose an hour of sleep. In autumn, they gain it back. The whole thing feels arbitrary because, frankly, it kind of is.

But DST has real consequences for anyone working across time zones. Meetings shift. Flight times get confusing. And for a few weeks each year, the time difference between countries changes in ways that catch people off guard.

What DST Actually Does

Daylight Saving Time moves clocks forward by one hour in spring and back by one hour in autumn. The idea is to shift an hour of daylight from the morning to the evening. Instead of sunrise at 5 AM and sunset at 7 PM, you get sunrise at 6 AM and sunset at 8 PM. More usable daylight after work.

The mnemonics: "Spring forward" (clocks go forward, you lose an hour) and "Fall back" (clocks go back, you gain an hour).

During DST, a time zone's UTC offset changes:

  • New York: UTC-5 (EST) → UTC-4 (EDT)
  • London: UTC+0 (GMT) → UTC+1 (BST)
  • Los Angeles: UTC-8 (PST) → UTC-7 (PDT)
  • Sydney: UTC+10 (AEST) → UTC+11 (AEDT)

A Brief History

Benjamin Franklin jokingly suggested in 1784 that Parisians could save candles by waking up earlier. But the modern concept was proposed independently by George Vernon Hudson (New Zealand, 1895) and William Willett (UK, 1907).

DST was first implemented during World War I. Germany and Austria-Hungary adopted it on April 30, 1916, to conserve coal. The US followed in 1918.

Since then, countries have adopted, abandoned, and re-adopted DST multiple times. The current US system (second Sunday in March, first Sunday in November) has been in place since 2007.

The EU voted to abolish DST in 2019, but the change has been indefinitely postponed. As of 2026, EU countries still observe DST.

The 3-Week Gap (and the 1-Week Gap)

In spring, the US switches on March 8 but Europe doesn't switch until March 29. During those 3 weeks, the US-Europe time difference is 1 hour less than usual. In autumn, Europe switches back on October 25 but the US doesn't switch until November 1 — creating a 1-week gap going the other direction.

This is the single most common source of DST-related scheduling errors. If you have a recurring call with European colleagues, the time shifts during these gap periods.

Countries That Don't Observe DST

Many of the world's most populous nations don't bother with DST:

  • China — Single time zone (UTC+8), no DST
  • India — UTC+5:30, never observed DST
  • Japan — Abolished DST in 1951
  • Russia — Abolished DST in 2014, now on permanent "summer time"
  • Most of Africa — No DST
  • Most of Southeast Asia — No DST

Within the US, Hawaii and Arizona (except the Navajo Nation) don't observe DST.

How to Avoid DST Mistakes

  1. Use IANA timezone names (e.g., America/New_York) in calendar tools. These handle DST automatically.
  2. Double-check recurring meetings during the weeks of March 8, March 29, October 25, and November 1.
  3. Avoid scheduling critical meetings during the 24 hours around a DST transition.
  4. Set a calendar reminder to review recurring meetings after each DST change.

FAQ

When do clocks change in 2026 in the US?

Spring forward: Sunday, March 8, 2026 at 2:00 AM. Fall back: Sunday, November 1, 2026 at 2:00 AM.

Does my phone automatically adjust?

Yes. All modern smartphones and computers automatically adjust for DST when your timezone is set to a geographic location rather than a fixed UTC offset.

Why doesn't everyone just abolish DST?

Many places are trying. The EU voted to end it in 2019 but hasn't implemented the change. The US Senate passed the Sunshine Protection Act in 2022 (which would make DST permanent), but it stalled in the House. Russia, Turkey, and several other countries have successfully abolished it.

Does DST affect UTC?

No. UTC never changes. DST only affects local time zones. UTC is always the same — which is why it's the standard for aviation, computing, and international coordination.
