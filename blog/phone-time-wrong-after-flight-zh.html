为什么您的手机在着陆后显示不同的时间（以及为什么这是正常的）

📅 July 21, 2026  ·  ⏱ 6 min read  ·  🏷 Travel, Time Zones, Mobile

You land in Berlin. Your iPhone says 10:42 AM. The airport screen says 10:42 AM. Everything matches. Then you check your hotel booking — wrong date. Or the meeting invite arrives with the wrong hour. You tap the time, look at the settings, and everything looks fine. So what's happening?

The honest answer is: probably nothing is wrong. Your phone is telling the truth — but time zones are a moving target, and there's a 30-second gap between landing and the phone's first signal that most people don't notice. Let's walk through what actually happens.

How Phones Pick a Time Zone

Modern phones (iOS and Android) don't know what time zone they're in by magic. They figure it out from one of three signals, in roughly this order:

  • Cell tower location. The carrier network tells the device which country and region it is in. This is the fastest signal — usually within a few seconds of landing and registering on a local network.
  • GPS coordinates. Once the GPS receiver gets a fix, it can resolve a time zone from the latitude/longitude using the on-device time zone database (the IANA tz database, baked into the operating system).
  • Your last manual override. If you've ever set "Set time zone automatically" to off, the phone keeps the last zone you picked — yours from home.

In practice, two of those three usually queue up within minutes of landing. The third is the cause of most "my phone is wrong" complaints.

The DST Trap Right After Spring or Autumn

If you fly during the month around a daylight saving transition, you can hit an awkward window: your home zone and the destination zone have both changed their clocks recently — or only one has. For example:

The phone does the right thing. The clock on the wall — and your body — are still catching up.

When the Phone Actually Gets It Wrong

There are a few real cases where the phone is the problem:

  • Airplane mode for the whole trip. No cell tower, no GPS update. The phone keeps your home zone until you turn airplane mode off and get a signal.
  • "Set Automatically" is off. Toggle found under Settings → General → Date & Time on iOS, or Settings → System → Date & Time on Android. If this is off, the time zone is whatever you last picked — usually your home zone forever.
  • Border regions. Crossing from France into Germany at the right moment can land you in the same time zone — but if the phone resolved via cell tower near the border, you might see the toggle fight the actual zone.
  • Outdated OS. Older iOS or Android versions have an old copy of the IANA time zone database and miss recent DST rule changes. For example, several countries (Russia, North Korea, Türkiye) changed their rules within the last decade. If your OS hasn't updated, you'll see the wrong offset.

How to Verify the Phone Is Right

The simplest test is to compare the phone against a known reference — not the airport clock (which can be stale). Pick two of these:

  • A public NTP time server. Many sites show "current UTC" with millisecond precision.
  • Another device on a different carrier. If two phones show the same minute and the airport screen shows the same minute, you're golden.
  • A reliable city clock on this site. Our city pages show the live time for any city worldwide, updated every second, using the same IANA database the phones do.

If all three agree, the phone isn't wrong — your head is just recalibrating after a long flight (which is most of the time).

If You Suspect a Bug

Try these in order:

  1. Toggle airplane mode on, wait 10 seconds, off.
  2. Settings → Date & Time → toggle "Set Automatically" off, then back on.
  3. Reboot the phone. This forces a fresh cell-tower and GPS resolution.
  4. Check the OS version and update if there's a pending update that includes "time zone database" or "DST changes" in the release notes.

If after all that the phone still disagrees with every other source, you may have a device-level bug. Report it to Apple or Google with the specific city and date — they collect these.
